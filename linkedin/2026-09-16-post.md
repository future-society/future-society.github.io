A government press release once took a policy team weeks to draft. Politico reported this week that the US executive branch wants that down to the speed of a prompt — LLMs drafting policy and processing citizen services across federal agencies, on the promise of government that runs "at the speed of software."

The article names the exact failure modes you'd expect: hallucinated legal precedent making it into drafted rules, "algorithmic capture" as agencies lean on a handful of proprietary models, and a "human-in-the-loop" review step that quietly turns into rubber-stamping once the volume gets high enough. Its sharpest point, though, is that the hardest parts of governing — equity, historical context, political sensitivity — are exactly where today's models are weakest. Speed and judgment are being traded off, and right now nobody is measuring the exchange rate.

That's not a hypothetical for us. Two FuSo papers test it directly. Clement Guitton, Vlada Druta, Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux, Estelle Hary, and Simon Mayer measured the actual social acceptability of automating administrative and legal processes in "Steering society through algorithms" (published in Big Data & Society) — asking people, not just policymakers, when they'll accept a machine in that seat. The research shows that people are far more likely to accept automated government decisions when they trust *who* is running the system, agree with *why* it is being used, know *where* the data came from, understand *what* sets the automated process in motion, and see that humans still retain *meaningful* control.

This is representative of the Technology Assessment work we do at @Future Society Hub at the @University of St.Gallen: building the evidence base that has to exist before "government at the speed of software" gets to call itself "good government", not just "fast government".
