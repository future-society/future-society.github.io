Let me start with a simple situation. Imagine that an important story breaks this morning. One person reads an article from a news organization. Another encounters a short clip in a social media feed. A third asks a chatbot: “What happened, and why does it matter?”

All three are trying to understand the same event. But they may see different facts, different voices, and different interpretations. In the feed, a recommender system helps decide what appears first and what remains unseen. In the chatbot, an AI system may bring several sources together into one confident sounding answer. And the clip itself may have been edited, translated, or even generated with AI.

Our information ecosystem has never stood still. News organizations remain important; social media has changed how stories spread and how people participate; and conversational AI is now becoming another way to seek information. These channels coexist and influence one another. AI increasingly shapes what reaches us and how we understand it.

Web search uses machine learning in ranking and retrieval; social media uses it in recommendation and moderation; chatbots rely on language models to generate responses. AI supports different functions across these services.

Selection · Generation · ConversationAI + Social Media

Let us begin with three functions: selection, generation, and conversation.

Selection is familiar from social media. A platform has far more posts and videos than any of us could see. Its recommender system chooses what to show, in what order, and to whom. Those choices may respond to our interests, but they also reflect what the platform measures and rewards. If it rewards time spent or repeated engagement, that shapes the information environment people experience.

Generation adds another layer. AI can help produce a post, an image, a voice recording, or a video. This can make expression and translation easier. It can also make misleading material easier to produce and harder to recognize.

Conversation changes the experience again. Rather than scrolling through posts or opening several links, we can ask a chatbot a question and receive a synthesized response. We might then ask it to explain an opposing view, summarize a controversy, or advise us what to do next. This can be genuinely useful. But the answer also depends on choices about sources, presentation, and what the system leaves out.

What does this mean for society?

One question is visibility. Whose experiences and arguments become easy to find? A recommender can help a small community reach people who need it. It can also repeatedly elevate a handful of voices or make a public debate appear more one-sided than it is.

A second question is trust. When a convincing image or account can be produced at scale, how do we decide what deserves our attention? And when a chatbot gives us a fluent answer, do we notice where the underlying evidence is uncertain or contested?

A third question is participation. Social media lets people speak, organize, learn, and find communities across borders. Conversational AI may make complicated issues more accessible and help people explore viewpoints they would not otherwise encounter. But we should ask whether these tools expand people’s ability to form their own judgments—or quietly narrow the range of perspectives they see.

These questions are especially vivid for young people. A young person may encounter advice about health, relationships, or politics in a feed, from a creator, or in a conversation with an AI system. The effects depend on the feature, the context, and the person using it. We need research that can tell us more than whether a service is simply “good” or “bad.”

That brings us to regulation. This is a global issue, and different places have responded in different ways.

In Europe, the Digital Services Act asks very large platforms to assess and mitigate systemic risks, including risks linked to their design and algorithmic systems. This raises demanding practical questions: How do we identify a risk that emerges across millions of users? What evidence should a platform provide? And how can independent researchers test its claims?

Other jurisdictions have put more emphasis on direct rules about access and use. Australia has introduced age restrictions for social media accounts. China has long imposed restrictions on minors’ online gaming and use. These approaches arise in very different legal and political settings, but they remind us that ideas about age, access, and product design are travelling across borders.

How do we assess systemic risks when information arrives as an answer?

The European Commission’s proposed EU KIDS Act makes that comparison particularly timely. It proposes age rules alongside more specific safety requirements for services children use, including social media and AI chatbots. It is still a proposal, and its measures deserve careful debate. We should ask what problem each rule addresses, whether it works, and what it may mean for children’s privacy, participation, and access to information.

There is another recent development that brings AI directly into this conversation. The European Commission has designated ChatGPT as a very large online search engine, or VLOSE, under the Digital Services Act. Reddit and Roblox were designated very large online platforms. ChatGPT’s designation does not mean that a chatbot and a social media feed work in the same way. It does mean that we now have to ask how obligations to assess and mitigate systemic risks apply when a service gives people an answer, rather than a ranked list of posts or links.

So our regulatory question is broader than “Which law applies?” It is: What would accountability look like for the actual systems people encounter each day?

This is why we need the three lenses in the same room.

The technical lens helps us ask what we can observe and measure. The social lens helps us decide which outcomes matter—and to whom. The regulatory lens helps us turn that evidence into duties, access, and meaningful ways to check whether interventions work.

This is also the motivation behind CoCoDa, our Swiss National Science Foundation project on the concentration of control and data in dominant online platforms. It brings legal and computer science research together to understand systemic risks and develop ways to investigate and address them. And it reflects the wider approach of the Future Society Hub: connecting analysis with building, deployment, and evaluation, so that our ideas can be tested in practice.

We will not resolve all these questions in one afternoon. But we can make them more precise. When someone says a recommender is transparent, transparent to whom, and about what? When a platform says it has reduced harm, what evidence would allow us to assess that claim? And as AI changes how people find and understand information, what do we want these systems to make possible for society?
