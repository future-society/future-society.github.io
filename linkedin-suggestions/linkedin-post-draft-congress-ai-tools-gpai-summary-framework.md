**Chosen news hook:** On 23 September 2026 (yesterday), NPR reported that
while Senate offices have free access to three basic AI chat interfaces —
Microsoft Copilot Chat, Google's Gemini Chat for Workspace Enterprise Plus,
and OpenAI's ChatGPT Enterprise — the Senate sergeant at arms has not
authorized any of the more advanced "agentic" tools, such as OpenAI's Codex
or Anthropic's Claude Code, that are simultaneously at the center of the AI
safety and regulatory debates Congress is trying to legislate. Adam
Kovacevich of the Chamber of Progress put it plainly: "Lawmakers writing
rules for technology they, in many cases, never even used — that's a
problem." Daniel Schuman of the American Governance Institute pushed back on
the security rationale behind the block. Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-OH) has
publicly described using an AI staffer, "Albert," for research, despite the
restrictions limiting what tools he can actually deploy. The story lands
while a majority of Americans tell pollsters they want AI regulated, but the
people drafting that regulation are structurally kept at arm's length from
the systems in question.

This is a direct hit on FuSo's "Technology Assessment" focus area — "advising
authorities on AI governance and regulatory expertise." NPR's story is about
a *capacity* problem: lawmakers can't personally test-drive frontier agentic
systems, so they're forced to regulate from the outside, on vendor claims and
secondhand accounts. That is exactly the gap Dick Blankvoort, Harshvardhan J.
Pandit and Maximilian Gahntz's "Quality Assessment of Public Summary of
Training Content for GPAI models required by AI Act Article 53(1)(d)" (ACM
FAccT, 2026) is built to close from the other direction: instead of asking
regulators to become hands-on power users of every model they oversee, it
gives them a structured framework to judge whether a provider's own mandated
disclosure is rigorous or just reassuring paperwork — oversight that doesn't
depend on Senate IT ever approving Claude Code.

## Draft post text

Senate staff can use ChatGPT Enterprise, Copilot Chat, and Gemini for Workspace. What they can't use — per NPR's reporting yesterday — is Codex, Claude Code, or any other agentic AI tool anywhere near the center of the AI safety debate Congress is actually trying to legislate.

"Lawmakers writing rules for technology they, in many cases, never even used — that's a problem," Adam Kovacevich of the Chamber of Progress told NPR. It's a fair point, and also an unfixable one on any timeline that matters: you cannot hands-on-test every frontier model fast enough to regulate the next one.

That's why oversight can't depend on lawmakers becoming power users. It has to depend on being able to judge, from the outside, whether what a provider hands you is a real disclosure or just polished paperwork. Dick Blankvoort, Harshvardhan J. Pandit, and Maximilian Gahntz built exactly that: a quality assessment framework for the public training-content summaries GPAI providers must now file under EU AI Act Article 53(1)(d) — a structured way to tell a rigorous summary from a hollow one, without needing a Senate-approved seat inside the model itself.

This is what Technology Assessment means at @Future Society Hub — FuSo, hosted at the @University of St.Gallen: building tools for oversight that scale independently of who has access to what, on either side of the Atlantic.
