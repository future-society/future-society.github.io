## Draft post text

California just did something no legislature has done before: it named "personalized feed" and "autoplay" as regulated features, not just regulated content.

On 10 September, Governor Newsom signed AB 1709, authored by Assemblymember Josh Lowenthal and sponsored by Common Sense Media, the Organization for Social Media Safety and the California Commission on the Status of Women and Girls. Starting 1 January 2027, platforms can no longer serve algorithmically personalized feeds or autoplay to verified users under 16 — the law calls these "psychologically exploitative features intended to maximize engagement that foreseeably lead to compulsive use." Penalties run up to $50,000 per affected minor. It passed both chambers unopposed.

That's a striking admission: personalization itself, not just what gets shown, is now treated as the design choice that needs regulating. Our Jannis Strecker, with Simon Mayer and Kenan Bektaş, reached the same conclusion from the research side a year earlier. Their paper "Towards Societally Beneficial Personalized Realities" (ACM DIS 2025, Honorable Mention) names what unconstrained personalization produces at scale: a loss of shared worlds and the emergence of perceptual filter bubbles — and argues that the fix isn't to reject personalization wholesale, but to build a conceptual foundation for *responsible* ubiquitous personalization systems, ones designed for the user's and society's benefit rather than for maximizing engagement.

California just legislated the failure mode for one age group and one platform category. The harder, longer-running question — the one our Personalized Reality research is built to answer — is what a personalization system has to look like, architecturally and ethically, to be trustworthy for everyone, on every surface where personalization is heading next: mixed reality, ambient computing, agentic assistants.

This is what "Inclusive and Healthy Society" means in our Focus Areas at Future Society Hub — FuSo, hosted at the University of St.Gallen: not just flagging the harms of personalization at scale, but designing the alternative.

To platform designers and regulators alike: is a categorical ban on personalization for minors the right unit of regulation, or does the real fix have to happen inside the personalization system itself?
