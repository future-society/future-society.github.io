**Chosen news hook:** On 30 September 2026, California Governor Gavin Newsom
signed a package of AI workplace bills, most notably **SB 947, the "No Robo
Bosses Act"** (authored by Sen. Jerry McNerney, sponsored by the California
Labor Federation, AFL-CIO) and **AB 1883** (Asm. Isaac Bryan). SB 947 bars
employers from relying solely on an automated decision system (ADS) to
discipline or fire a worker — a human reviewer must independently corroborate
the ADS output before any such action, and ADS can't be used to infer a
worker's protected characteristics or to retaliate against someone exercising
a legal right. It takes effect 1 July 2027, and is a revised version of a
bill Newsom himself vetoed in 2025 — CNBC's coverage headlined it as Newsom
"reversing his earlier veto." AB 1883 separately bans AI-driven workplace
"surveillance tools" that predict or infer an employee's emotional state, or
that collect "neural data" (information about activity in the central or
peripheral nervous system) — effective immediately on signing. Newsom's
office quoted him saying "AI should expand opportunity – not come at the
expense of workers and families." Lorena Gonzalez, president of the
California Labor Federation, AFL-CIO and SB 947's lead sponsor, put it more
sharply: "We cannot have workplaces where humans are fired by AI or face
discipline from an algorithm. Governor Newsom can protect the dignity of
work by signing the No Robo Bosses Act."

This is a direct match for FuSo's "Technology Assessment" focus area
(regulatory expertise on emergent technologies) and connects to two

what Lorena Gonzalez invoked for AB 1883/SB 947, and which speaks directly to
why reducing a worker to an inferred emotional or neural signal is a dignity
harm, not just a privacy one.

## Draft post text

California just told employers: if an algorithm wants to fire someone, a human has to actually check its work first.

On 30 September, Governor Gavin Newsom signed SB 947, the "No Robo Bosses Act" — a bill he vetoed in a different form just last year. Starting 1 July 2027, employers can no longer rely solely on an automated decision system to discipline or terminate a worker: a human reviewer must independently corroborate the machine's output first, and the system can't be used to infer protected characteristics or punish someone for exercising a legal right. The same signing included AB 1883, effective immediately, which bans AI workplace tools that predict an employee's emotional state or collect their "neural data" outright.

Lorena Gonzalez, president of the California Labor Federation, AFL-CIO and the bill's lead sponsor, framed it in exactly the terms that matter here: "We cannot have workplaces where humans are fired by AI or face discipline from an algorithm... Governor Newsom can protect the dignity of work." Dignity, not just accuracy, is the stake.

That's the precise question two of our papers test empirically rather than rhetorically. Clément Guitton, Vlada Druta, Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux, Estelle Hary and Simon Mayer's "Steering society through algorithms" (Big Data & Society) measures when people actually find it acceptable for a machine to make an administrative or legal decision about them — the exact line SB 947 just drew for employment. And Maud Reveilhac, Vlada Druta, Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux and Marlène Sapin's "Unpacking social scoring systems and their impact on fairness and digital dignity" (Big Data & Society) names the harm AB 1883 is reaching for before it has fully been named in policy circles: being reduced to an inferred score or signal is a dignity harm, independent of whether the inference is even accurate.

California wrote one hard-won rule for one domain. The underlying question — where does a human have to stay the final word, and what do we lose in dignity when they don't — is the one @Future Society Hub, hosted at the @University of St.Gallen, is building the evidence base for across domains, not just employment.

If your organization is drafting policy on automated decision-making in the workplace — or anywhere a machine's output used to end with a human's sign-off — we'd like to compare notes.
