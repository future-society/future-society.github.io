## Suggested post text

This week, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei publicly called for binding,
FAA-style regulation of frontier AI: mandatory third-party testing before
release, with government power to block deployment if a model fails. Rahm
Emanuel's reaction — he's never seen an industry ask to be regulated before.
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's reaction — slow down. The US is now
arguing over whether such a regime should exist at all.

The EU is already running a version of it — and that experience shows why
"mandatory disclosure" is only half the answer.

Since August 2026, Article 53(1)(d) of the EU AI Act has required providers
of general-purpose AI models to publish public summaries of their training
content. It's the same instinct behind Amodei's proposal: if regulators and
the public can see what went into a model, they can assess its risks.

But do these summaries actually deliver transparency, or just paperwork?
Our PhD candidate Dick Blankvoort, together with Harshvardhan J. Pandit and
Maximilian Gahntz, built a quality assessment framework to find out —
scoring GPAI public summaries on whether they're clear, comprehensive, and
genuinely usable by the people they're meant to inform (ACM FAccT 2026).

Their finding is the uncomfortable part of any "FAA for AI" proposal: a
disclosure mandate is only as strong as the quality bar enforced behind it.
Before the US writes a new rule, it's worth looking at how well a similar
one is already working in the EU.

This is exactly FuSo's Technology Assessment focus: moving the conversation
from "should AI be regulated" to "how do we make AI regulation actually
work."

Paper: https://doi.org/10.1145/3805689.3806755
