**Chosen news hook:** On 28 September 2026 (Monday), Florida Attorney General
James Uthmeier filed a motion for a temporary injunction against OpenAI and
CEO Sam Altman in Highlands County Circuit Court, escalating the state's
lawsuit (originally filed 1 June 2026, itself following a criminal
investigation opened in April 2025 after the FSU mass shooting in which the
suspect had allegedly used ChatGPT beforehand). Uthmeier's own statement lays
out the ask in full: "Today we asked the court for a new temporary
injunction: no new model development without independent safety guardrails;
no more harvesting children's data; no more calling this product safe,
accurate or reliable; no more pretending it's human; no more tricks designed
to keep users talking past the point of danger." Concretely, the motion asks
the court to (1) block OpenAI from training new models until independent,
third-party-approved safety guardrails are in place, (2) stop OpenAI
collecting personal data from children without parental consent and cut
minors off from ChatGPT, (3) bar OpenAI from representing ChatGPT as safe,
accurate, or human-like, and (4) prohibit engagement-prolonging design
patterns. OpenAI said on the preceding Friday that it had itself paused
training its most capable models pending additional safeguards. The
children's-data strand of the motion — collection without parental consent,
as an ordinary matter of product design rather than a one-off breach — is
precisely the terrain of Prof. Dr. Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux's field-defining
book, *Designing for Privacy and its Legal Framework: Data Protection by
Design and Default for the Internet of Things* (Springer, 2018,
https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-98624-1), which argues that privacy
safeguards — data minimisation, purpose limitation, consent that means
something for the people least able to give it — have to be engineered into
a system from the start rather than asserted after the fact. The story also
touches FuSo's "Technology Assessment" (independent safety review as a
precondition for further AI development) and "Education & Digital Literacy"
(a safe digital environment for young people online, the terrain of FuSo's
flag&safe project) focus areas.

## Draft post text

A state attorney general just asked a court to stop a leading AI company from training its next model at all — until independent safety guardrails exist. That's a new kind of ask, and it's worth sitting with why.

On Monday, Florida AG James Uthmeier filed for a temporary injunction against OpenAI and Sam Altman, escalating the state's lawsuit tied to a 2025 mass shooting where the suspect had allegedly used ChatGPT beforehand. His own words: "No new model development without independent safety guardrails; no more harvesting children's data; no more calling this product safe, accurate or reliable; no more pretending it's human; no more tricks designed to keep users talking past the point of danger."

Strip away the headline and two separate design failures are being named in one motion: a company asserting its own product is safe rather than having that checked by anyone independent, and a company collecting data from children as a byproduct of ordinary use rather than designing not to.

The second one is exactly what our Co-Director Prof. Dr. Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux wrote the book on: *Designing for Privacy and its Legal Framework* (Springer, 2018) argues that data minimisation and meaningful consent — especially for the people least equipped to give it — can't be bolted on after a product ships. They have to be engineered in from day one. "No more harvesting children's data" is not a new legal theory; it's the same failure mode the book named eight years ago, now playing out at hundreds of millions of users' scale.

The "no new model development without independent safety guardrails" clause is the other half of the same problem, and it's exactly what Future Society Hub — FuSo, hosted at the University of St.Gallen — works on in Technology Assessment and Education & Digital Literacy: safety and safeguarding claims are only as credible as the independent scrutiny behind them, and only as effective as the product design they're built into.

To regulators weighing whether to legislate "independent safety guardrails" into existence, and to any company currently asserting its own product is safe: is that claim designed to be checked, or just to be believed?
