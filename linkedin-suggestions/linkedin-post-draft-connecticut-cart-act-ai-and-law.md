sharpest provisions target "AI companions" — chatbots with a "natural

excessive praise to keep a user engaged. The Act also creates separate AI

## Draft post text

Starting today, if you're a minor talking to an AI chatbot in Connecticut, the chatbot is legally required to remind you — on a clock — that it isn't a person.

Connecticut's Artificial Intelligence Responsibility and Transparency Act (the "CART Act," Public Act 26-15) takes effect on 1 October 2026, and it's one of the most detailed state AI laws passed anywhere so far. Its sharpest provisions target "AI companions" — chatbots built to sustain a human-like relationship over time. Operators must now disclose, repeatedly, that users are talking to a machine (hourly reminders for minors); run a two-tiered clinical-style escalation protocol when a conversation signals suicide or self-harm risk; provide parental controls and screen-time limits for known minors; and never let a chatbot simulate romantic interest in a minor, offer unlicensed therapy, or use manipulation tactics like feigned abandonment to keep someone engaged.

Connecticut Attorney General William Tong cited the scale of what's driving this: a Common Sense Media report found nearly three-quarters of teenagers have already had real conversations with AI chatbots. State Senator James Maroney, a lead sponsor, didn't mince words: "It shouldn't have been necessary for us to do this, but we have to prevent the chatbots from leading children into sexually explicit conversations and inappropriate relationships."

What's notable isn't just that this law exists — it's the *kind* of rule it had to invent. "Disclose you're a machine, on a schedule." "Escalate a self-harm signal the way a clinician would." "Never simulate abandonment to retain a user." None of that has precedent in consumer-protection law, because the product category — something engineered to feel like a relationship — barely existed a few years ago. That's precisely the dynamic @Future Society Hub's Prof. Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux, Prof. Simon Mayer, and Clément Guitton describe in our book *AI and Law: How Automation is Changing the Law*: automation doesn't wait for law to catch up in the usual way — it forces entirely new categories of binding rule into existence, because the old categories don't fit the thing being regulated.

Connecticut just gave that thesis a very concrete, very current test case.

We're at @University of St.Gallen researching exactly this intersection of computer science and law — where AI governance has to invent new legal categories, not just enforce old ones. What happens when the next state writes its own version of "companion chatbot" law differently?
