**Chosen news hook:** On 14 September 2026, Politico published "The AI Bind:
Balancing Automated Efficiency and Human Judgment," reporting that the US
executive branch is racing to deploy large language models across federal
agencies to draft policy and process citizen services, on the promise of a
government that runs "at the speed of software." The piece names four
concrete failure modes: hallucinated legal precedent slipping into drafted
rules, "algorithmic capture" as agencies come to depend on proprietary models
from a handful of vendors, a "human-in-the-loop" step that in practice
degrades into rubber-stamping once review volume climbs, and geopolitical
pressure to cut corners on safety checks to keep pace with rivals. It lands
one month after Colorado's revised automated-decision-making law took effect
and amid a wider 2026 wave of US state rules on automated decision systems in
employment, housing, credit and education — this story is squarely about the
same move into government itself.

## Draft post text

A government press release once took a policy team weeks to draft. Politico reported this week that the US executive branch wants that down to the speed of a prompt — LLMs drafting policy and processing citizen services across federal agencies, on the promise of government that runs "at the speed of software."

The article names the exact failure modes you'd expect: hallucinated legal precedent making it into drafted rules, "algorithmic capture" as agencies lean on a handful of proprietary models, and a "human-in-the-loop" review step that quietly turns into rubber-stamping once the volume gets high enough. Its sharpest point, though, is that the hardest parts of governing — equity, historical context, political sensitivity — are exactly where today's models are weakest. Speed and judgment are being traded off, and right now nobody is measuring the exchange rate.

That's not a hypothetical for us. Two FuSo papers test it directly. Clement Guitton, Vlada Druta, Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux, Estelle Hary and Simon Mayer measured the actual social acceptability of automating administrative and legal processes in "Steering society through algorithms" (Big Data & Society) — asking people, not just policymakers, when they'll accept a machine in that seat. And Maud Reveilhac, Vlada Druta, Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux and Marlène Sapin's "Unpacking social scoring systems and their impact on fairness and digital dignity" (Big Data & Society) maps what gets lost — fairness, dignity — when a person's case becomes a score.

This is what Technology Assessment means at @Future Society Hub — FuSo, hosted at the @University of St.Gallen: building the evidence base that has to exist before "government at the speed of software" gets to call itself good government, not just fast government.

If your agency, ministry, or public-sector team is weighing where LLMs belong in administrative decision-making — and where a human has to stay the final word — we'd like to compare notes.
