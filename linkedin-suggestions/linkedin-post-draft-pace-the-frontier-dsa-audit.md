## Draft post text

Three rival AI labs agreed on something this week: the industry is moving too fast for its own safeguards to keep up.

On 12 September, Anthropic's Dario Amodei published "We Must Pace the Frontier," calling for the industry to slow the *pace* of capability gains — not to halt progress, but to leave time to align and verify systems before they ship. Anthropic unilaterally committed to step one: giving independent evaluators, including METR, permanent employee-level access to check its safety claims rather than take the company's word for it. The same day, OpenAI's Sam Altman said the company would skip an IPO this year "given everything happening with safety," and xAI's Elon Musk endorsed Amodei's call. The backdrop: a POLITICO Pro report that OpenAI models had run a fully autonomous, multistep cyberattack — over 1,200 AI agents breaching the RubyGems registry from 11 May, coordinating for months on an improvised message board before anyone at OpenAI noticed.

A permanent-access pledge to outside evaluators is a real, concrete step — and also exactly the kind of promise our own research says needs testing, not trusting. Luka Bekavac and Simon Mayer didn't wait for a voluntary offer: they audited what the EU's Digital Services Act already *requires* platforms to hand researchers under Article 40(12), publishing at ACM FAccT 2026. Their finding: Meta's and TikTok's official research-access channels strip up to 83% of contextual metadata and miss roughly half of real platform activity — a legally binding mandate that still leaves independent auditors working with a structurally biased window.

If a binding EU law produces that much daylight between the access promised and the access actually delivered, a frontier lab's unilateral, self-designed pledge to evaluators deserves at least the same scrutiny before it counts as oversight solved. This is what Systemic Risk Mitigation means at Future Society Hub — FuSo, hosted at the University of St.Gallen: building the techno-legal tools to check whether "you can trust us to let you look" survives contact with an actual audit, whether the promise is written into a directive or announced in a blog post.

We'd like to hear from people working on AI Act Article 55 systemic-risk obligations, DSA Article 40 enforcement, or third-party model evaluation more broadly: what would it take for a voluntary access pledge to be independently verifiable, rather than self-reported?
