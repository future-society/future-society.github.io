## Draft post text

Australia just proposed something most platforms have spent a decade arguing is impossible: a working "off" switch for your algorithm.

On 8 September, the Albanese Government released draft Digital Duty of Care legislation — "My Feed, My Way" — that would require every major social media platform to let users over 16 opt out of personalised, algorithmically ranked feeds and see a plain chronological feed of accounts they actually follow instead. Separately, games, apps and AI chatbots would have to protect under-18s from design features shown to harm self-esteem or drive compulsive use. Non-compliance could cost platforms up to A$109.2 million. It follows Australia's under-16 social media account ban and lands the same week an Australian minister warned of a "global reckoning" for Big Tech.

An opt-out is only meaningful if regulators, researchers and users can actually verify what it changes. That's the exact gap our Luka Bekavac, together with Jannis Strecker-Bischoff, Kimberly Garcia, Simon Mayer and Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux, built tooling for: SOAP, an open-source framework for auditing personalised recommender systems on VLOPs using sock-puppet accounts, published in ACM Transactions on Recommender Systems. It empirically compares what a platform's ranking algorithm actually surfaces to different simulated users — precisely the kind of independent check that turns "you can opt out" from a policy promise into a testable claim.

This is what Systemic Risk Mitigation means at @Future Society Hub — FuSo, hosted at the @University of St.Gallen: building the techno-legal tools to hold platforms like @Meta, @TikTok, @Google and @X to account for what their algorithms do, not just what their settings pages say they do.

If Australia's opt-out becomes law, who verifies it actually works? We'd like to hear from people inside eSafety-style regulators, platform trust & safety teams, and researchers auditing recommender systems elsewhere in the world.
